## Finding 1
A guide on ADA compliance for restaurant websites, last updated September 10, 2026, says a cafe owner stays legally responsible for accessibility even when online ordering runs on a third-party platform: Title III of the ADA covers discrimination 'directly, or through contractual, licensing, or other arrangements,' so handing checkout to a delivery marketplace or reservation widget does not move the obligation off the cafe's site. The practical target is WCAG 2.2 Level AA, applied to the menu, the ordering flow, the reservation form, and the hours and location page. The most common failure points are pale gray prices on light backgrounds (WebAIM's February 2026 report found low-contrast text on 83.9% of food and drink home pages), unlabeled form fields, keyboard traps in modifier dialogs, and PDF-only menus: a menu exported from a design tool is usually a picture of text with no reading order, so the fix is an HTML menu as the canonical version with any PDF kept only as a download. Three steps are recommended before a vendor contract renews: ask the ordering vendor for a current accessibility conformance report, test the embedded flow on the cafe's own domain rather than the vendor's demo, and put a staffed phone alternative on the page. Automated scanners catch only about 30 to 57 percent of issues, so the remaining check is a manual keyboard and screen-reader run through the whole ordering path.
- https://wcagc.com/blog/ada-compliance-restaurants (published 2026-09-10)

## Finding 2
A restaurant SEO guide from RestoLabs (the page states no publication date) advises that a cafe website should carry its menu as a text HTML page, not only as a PDF, with dish names, prices, descriptions, dietary labels, and clear sections; a PDF can be offered as a download but should never be the only format because it is harder to use on mobile and harder for search engines to read. Conversion guidance says the key actions, order online, call, and location, should sit above the fold on both mobile and desktop. For discovery beyond Google, the guide cites SOCi's 2026 Local Visibility Index: AI assistants recommend far fewer local businesses than Google, with only 1.2% of locations appearing in ChatGPT, 11% in Gemini, and 7.4% in Perplexity, versus 35.9% in Google's local 3-pack. The signals that make a cafe legible to those tools are consistency of name, address, hours, menu details, and ordering links across the web, plus clear website content: cuisine, menu, delivery options, and FAQs stated in plain text so search engines and AI tools can verify them.
- https://restolabs.com/blog/restaurant-seo (undated)

## Finding 3
A 16-minute guide on digital menu design published August 12, 2026 argues the high-converting cafe menu is deliberately boring: aim for a 4.5 to 1 text-to-background contrast ratio, keep body text at 16px or larger, use one accent color reserved for things to tap (the add button, an active category chip), and give each dish generous white space because empty space makes an item feel more expensive. Prices should be the same size as the dish name with no currency symbol, no dotted leader lines, and never stacked in a neat right-hand column, citing a Cornell study that found guests spent noticeably more with plain numerals than with '$24.00' style pricing. Categories should hold 6 to 8 dishes each because a long list gets skimmed and a short one gets read, and on mobile the most valuable real estate is whatever loads before the first scroll, so the best-margin items belong near the top. Photos are all-or-nothing per category: if three dishes have photos and five do not, the five will underperform, and a bad photo costs more than no photo. Anything tappable should be at least 44px tall, and the piece warns against serving the menu as a PDF, calling it a poster you have to pinch and drag instead of a real digital menu.
- https://medium.com/@tabres.com/digital-menu-design-colors-fonts-and-spacing-that-sell-more-2026-tabres-6820a8d63d13 (published 2026-08-12)
