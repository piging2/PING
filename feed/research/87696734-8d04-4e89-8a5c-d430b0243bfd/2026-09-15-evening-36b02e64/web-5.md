Beat: web
Angle: List what a small contractor using AI call or chat answering must disclose and get consent for today under current FCC/FTC rules and Colorado's proposed chatbot rules (Oct 26 hearing; no hearing today). Do not re-report the hearing date; mark each item proposal vs enforceable. Stake: PING Social deploys AI answering and must pitch it as compliant.

## Finding 1
FCC and TCPA layer. ENFORCEABLE: the FCC's declaratory ruling FCC 24-17 (released Feb 8, 2024) confirms that the TCPA's restrictions on 'artificial or prerecorded voice' cover current AI technologies that generate human voices, including voice cloning and AI systems that simulate conversation with callers. That triggers three duties for outbound calls: (1) prior express consent of the called party before placing the call, absent an emergency or exemption, with the FCC's rules requiring prior express written consent when the message is telemarketing (47 CFR 64.1200); (2) every artificial or prerecorded voice message must state clearly at the beginning of the message the identity of the business responsible for initiating the call; (3) telemarketing messages must offer specified opt-out methods. The ruling states these requirements apply to 'any AI technology that initiates any outbound telephone call using an artificial or prerecorded voice to consumers.' The outbound framing is the key line for AI answering: a contractor's AI that answers inbound calls is not initiating an outbound call, so the TCPA consent regime does not attach to the answering itself. It does attach to outbound uses such as AI-voice callbacks and missed-call text-backs, and the FCC treats text messages as calls. PROPOSAL: the FCC's August 2024 notice of proposed rulemaking (CG Docket No. 23-362) would add AI-specific duties, requiring consent forms to clearly and conspicuously disclose that consent covers AI-generated voice or text and requiring an AI-use disclosure at the beginning of each AI-generated call. That notice was never adopted as a final rule, so those items are not enforceable today.

## Finding 2
The written-consent piece of the TCPA regime is now contested law. ENFORCEABLE (within their jurisdictions): in Bradford v. Sovereign Pest the Fifth Circuit set aside the FCC's rule requiring prior express written consent for autodialed or artificial/prerecorded telemarketing calls, holding the TCPA's text requires only 'the prior express consent of the called party,' which includes oral consent; on March 20, 2026 the U.S. District Court for the District of Maryland reached the same conclusion in Bradley v. DentalPlans.com. Both decisions rest on Loper Bright's end of Chevron deference to FCC interpretations of the TCPA. The practical read from the Perkins Coie analysis: businesses in the Fifth Circuit (Texas, Louisiana, Mississippi) and increasingly elsewhere may rely on oral consent, but the FCC's written-consent rule remains on the books outside those decisions and the law is fluid, so companies should weigh the jurisdictional implications of where they operate. For the pitch, documented written consent remains the safe harbor to promise even where courts now accept oral consent.

## Finding 3
FTC layer. ENFORCEABLE: Section 5 of the FTC Act prohibits unfair or deceptive acts or practices, and the FTC polices AI through that authority, so an AI answering agent must not mislead a caller in a material way, for example by letting a caller reasonably believe they are speaking with a human employee when that belief matters. Also ENFORCEABLE: the FTC's Rule on Impersonation of Government and Businesses, finalized in 2024, prohibits falsely implying affiliation with a business; an AI agent that identifies itself as the contractor's AI assistant and names the business it answers for is authorized representation, while one that poses as a specific human staffer risks impersonation exposure. PROPOSAL: on July 7, 2026 the FTC published a proposed policy statement on AI output accuracy that sets the agency's disclosure bar at 'clear and conspicuous,' prominent and persistent, placed where users will actually see it, with one-time fine-print disclaimers deemed insufficient. As a proposed statement it is not enforceable, but it signals how the FTC will judge AI disclosures. The same statement asserts that state AI laws requiring undisclosed output changes can conflict with Section 5, putting the FTC in public tension with Colorado's AI regime.

## Finding 4
Colorado layer, all PROPOSAL or not-yet-effective. The Chatbot Safety Act (HB 26-1263), signed May 2026 and effective January 1, 2027, targets general-purpose, consumer-facing conversational AI services offered to the public, imposing disclosure, age-estimation, minor-protection, crisis-response, and annual-reporting duties on covered operators. The Attorney General's proposed implementing rules (4 CCR 904-6), filed August 11, 2026, would spell out the operating detail: how and when the 'you are talking to an AI' disclosure appears, including refreshes during long sessions; age-assurance methods that go beyond self-declaration; safeguards for minors and suicide/self-harm crisis protocols; controls implementing the ban on presenting chatbot output as provided by, endorsed by, or equivalent to a licensed health-care, legal, or mental-health professional; and annual reports to the AG with user tiers, with reporting starting July 1, 2027. None of this is enforceable today: the statute takes effect January 1, 2027 and the rules are still proposed. Two scoping notes for the pitch: there is no small-business exemption in either the statutes or the proposed rules, and Seyfarth's analysis notes the Act targets general-purpose public-facing services while narrow, task-specific bots limited to bounded functions (like a contractor's own call-answering agent) may fall outside it. Disclosing the AI's nature up front satisfies both the proposed Colorado standard and the existing federal deception standard, so it is the compliant pitch either way.
