## Finding 1
A 2026 comparison of nine cafe POS systems, tested for coffee-counter realities like the fee on small-ticket sales, drink modifier speed, tipping, and loyalty, names Square the best overall for small and growing cafes: the software plan is free at $0 per month with 2.6 percent plus 15 cents per in-person transaction, terminals from about $299 and a $59 card reader option; online orders cost 3.3 percent plus 30 cents on the free plan or 2.9 percent plus 30 cents on paid plans, and the Plus plan is $49 per month. Business News Daily, in its own 2026 coffee-shop POS review, calls Square the best pick for new coffee shops because it is free to start, includes a free swipe reader, supports offline payments, integrates with over 200 apps, and charges $45 per month for its loyalty add-on. Toast is the pick for high-volume and multi-location cafes: $0 Starter or $69 per month Point of Sale, 2.49 percent plus 15 cents in person on the paid plan, and spill- and heat-resistant hardware from about $799, but it offers no free trial, locks the shop into Toast payment processing, and usually comes with multi-year contracts and early termination fees. Clover runs $135 or more per month on the restaurant plan with 2.3 to 3.5 percent plus 10 cents, but contracts typically run 36 months and pricing varies by reseller. UpMenu prices its cafe POS at $29 per month per terminal with 0 percent commission on direct orders (online ordering from $49 per month), runs on iPad with a terminal from $390, and discloses on the page that UpMenu is the reviewer's own platform. Lightspeed Restaurant starts at $69 per month ($189 Essential) with 2.6 percent plus 10 cents and the deepest ingredient-level inventory, aimed at multi-location cafes. SpotOn offers a $0 Quick Start with hardware included and 1.99 percent plus 25 cents. The UpMenu review cites client Michelangelo 301, which saw 51 percent of its online orders come through its own branded app and saved nearly $130,000 in delivery commissions in a single year. The UpMenu page states no explicit publication date; its listing showed it last updated 83 days before the reading day.

## Finding 2
An August 7, 2026 Foodtech Family piece reports US quick-service restaurants are rethinking third-party delivery in 2026: DoorDash-style platforms charge 15 to 30 percent per order before Boost fees, promotional placements, and refund disputes, with total delivery costs easily exceeding 30 to 40 percent of revenue, a problem serious enough that the FTC proposed regulatory action against third-party delivery charges in 2024. Digital orders are expected to make up more than half of all QSR sales, and the working strategy the piece documents is a two-channel one: aggregators for reach and acquisition, the shop's own channel for retention and loyalty, because a guest who orders twice through a third-party app is still unknown to the shop while a loyalty customer returns to the shop's own app where it keeps the margin. The piece cites the National Restaurant Association: quick-service restaurants generate roughly 71 percent of sales from repeat customers, and loyalty members visit 20 percent more often and spend 20 percent more per check; loyalty program enrollment reached 48 percent of diners in 2025 and loyalty-influenced transactions grew 28.5 percent year over year. PYMNTS research cited there finds nearly two-thirds of restaurant delivery decisions are now influenced by loyalty programs. On the fee side, a joe.coffee pricing explainer for coffee shops (page states no publication date) puts the floor for card-not-present processing at 2.9 percent plus 30 cents, which on a $4 latte means the 30-cent flat fee alone is 7.5 percent and total processing lands at about 10.4 percent before any monthly fees, setup costs, or loyalty program costs; it warns many shops end up paying over 20 percent all-in for basic online and mobile ordering. A Restolabs 2026 report (page states no publication date) finds American concepts run about 55 percent pickup and that 58 percent of customers prefer ordering directly from a restaurant's own site or app.

## Finding 3
Business News Daily's 2026 coffee-shop POS review shows loyalty is usually an add-on rather than included: Square's loyalty program costs an additional $45 per month; TouchBistro's CRM-driven loyalty is a $99 per month add-on; Clover includes loyalty starting with its Standard plan; Toast's loyalty is a custom-priced add-on; and most vendors stack further fees for marketing tools and online ordering on top of monthly software tiers of $59 to $200. A May 2, 2026 announcement for Passtastic describes an approach gaining ground with independent cafes: digital stamp cards and points delivered through Apple Wallet and Google Wallet, already installed on most phones, so customers join with no app download while the shop gets location-triggered notifications, automated campaigns, and real-time loyalty analytics; one early user, the Bucharest cafe Rewind, enrolled 80 percent of its customers in the digital stamp card in its first season to keep coffee regulars through the slower winter months. A Square App Marketplace product called Subport turns regulars into subscribers with drink plans such as a $30 per month daily-drip plan; one Austin cafe signed 120 customers in the first month, about $3,600 in upfront revenue, and around 40 percent of redeemers added a food item, roughly $1,100 in extra add-on sales. That Subport example is vendor-supplied and its page states no publication date.

## Finding 4
On September 8, 2026, DeskPanther launched operations software for independent food and beverage shops, built not by a software company but by a coffee operator: founder Jade Valore co-founded Rad Coffee in 2015 and has run multi-location coffee shops since. The pitch is that the POS knows what was sold and the scheduler knows who was working, but neither knows the sink is leaking: everything between lives in group texts, paper logs, sticky notes, and the owner's memory, including inventory counts, ordering, out-of-stock items, repairs, cash drops, time corrections, announcements, and shift handoffs, and the tools that do exist were built and priced for chains rather than two- or four-store operators. DeskPanther is explicitly not a POS, does not connect to one, and is not a scheduler (Valore still runs Sling across her stores); it covers the operational work around the register, with employees recording conditions from the floor on any phone and managers and owners acting on and viewing them across locations. At Rad Coffee, a weekly inventory-ordering workflow that took 60 to 90 minutes now typically takes under 20 minutes, and low-stock requests arrive with a timestamp, sender, view, and ship status so the guesswork is gone. More than 200 businesses joined the pre-launch waitlist, including over 100 ice cream shops. Pricing is per location rather than per employee: $39 per location per month for a single shop, $34 at two or three locations, and $29 at four or more, applied automatically; mobile vendors such as trucks and carts run on the same app; it is available to US shops with a 30-day trial, and the company is bootstrapped and self-funded by its founder.
