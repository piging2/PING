Beat: web
Angle: Report the Colorado winter 2026-2027 outlook and what it implies for Western Slope HVAC and plumbing demand and homeowner urgency over the next 60 days, framed for a consultant pitching missed-call capture. This was asked today for 'Billed for ten' but never published as a story; file only the forecast and its business implication.

## Finding 1
The official forecasters agree on a historically strong El Nino for the 2026-2027 winter. NOAA's Climate Prediction Center says El Nino is strengthening with a greater than 90 percent chance of a very strong, super event this fall and winter, and a 69 percent chance that in October through December the El Nino conditions are the strongest since at least 1950. The CPC's seasonal precipitation outlook map, published August 20, 2026, gives parts of Colorado a 33 to 50 percent likelihood of heavier than normal rain or snow from October through December, with parts of California, Arizona, Nevada, Utah and New Mexico shaded the same way. For December through February, the CPC outlook leans toward above-average snowfall for southern Colorado, most of the Front Range and the Eastern Plains, with equal chances of above or below normal temperatures; northwest Colorado gets an equal-chances call. El Nino shifts the jet stream south, which favors more storm activity for the southern tier. Meteorologist Matt Makens, in an August 29 video, put the Southwest and Southern Rockies as this winter's biggest snow winners, while warning that a historic-strength event has no precedent data and adds uncertainty.

## Finding 2
The winter is forecast as wet but mild, which matters for low-elevation valley towns on the Western Slope. The Old Farmer's Almanac winter 26/27 forecast covers Colorado and most of the Intermountain West with the descriptor 'Mild, Snowy,' calls conditions 'wet' across the region, and notes some areas could see 'heavier-than-normal' snowfall. Meteorologist Chris Tomer's 2026-27 forecast says there is a very strong chance the super El Nino brings higher than average temperatures, so the rain/snow line creeps higher and much precipitation falls as rain instead of snow at lower elevations. OpenSnow's Alan Smith, quoted in the Coloradoan, says above-normal snowfall is favored in the southern mountains (Telluride, Silverton, Purgatory, Wolf Creek) in strong/super El Nino years, with the Front Range foothills and cities often seeing above-average snowfall from the southerly storm track, but snowfall weighted toward the early season: above-average favored October through December and in March and April, with below-average midseason in January and February. The practical read: valley floors get more of the season's moisture as rain, while cold snaps still arrive, which is the freeze-thaw pattern that bursts pipes and splits irrigation lines.

## Finding 3
The storm season starts inside the 60-day window. AccuWeather meteorologist Brandon Buckingham says the central and southern Rockies are among the areas that should see an early arrival of more typical fall weather, driven by El Nino's more active subtropical jet stream producing a higher frequency of storms, and that more frequent cold fronts are expected into the latter half of September and in October, bringing a higher frequency of cooldowns. Colorado state climatologist Russ Schumacher notes that fall and spring snowstorms are more likely in Colorado in El Nino years, and that the last three strong El Ninos (2015-16, 1997-98 and 1982-83) favored above-average snow for the same southern-mountain and Front Range areas flagged this time. Peaks2Plains Weather chief meteorologist Matt Meister adds that the CPC's September-through-November outlook favors above-average precipitation across parts of Colorado with a stronger signal in the Southwest, and that along the Front Range, eastern plains and areas south of I-70, El Nino can raise the odds of a memorable, high-impact storm, citing the 1982 Christmas blizzard, the October 1997 blizzard, December 2006 storms and the 2019 bomb cyclone as strong-El-Nino precedents.

## Finding 4
The business implication for the missed-call capture pitch, grounded in the forecast: Western Slope HVAC and plumbing demand concentrates into spikes, and the next 60 days are when those spikes start. Homeowners buy furnace tune-ups and winterization in September and early October, then the first cold fronts of late September and October trigger no-heat emergency calls, and storm-driven freeze-thaw cycles trigger burst-pipe calls. With El Nino giving above-normal precipitation odds for October through December, an active early-season storm track, and 'mild' storms that deliver rain at valley elevations followed by cold snaps, this fall carries above-normal odds of both no-heat nights and freeze damage weeks. That is exactly the contractor's worst phone problem: calls spike during storms and after hours, technicians are on roofs and under houses, and the homeowner with a flooded crawlspace or a dead furnace calls whoever answers first. The consultant's line is that a super El Nino fall makes 'every call gets answered' a revenue argument, not a nice-to-have, because the season's busiest, highest-value call weeks land inside this 60-day window.
