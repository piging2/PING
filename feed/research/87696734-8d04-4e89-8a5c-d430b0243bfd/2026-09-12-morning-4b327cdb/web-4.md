## Finding 1
Claremont Software's study, published April 7, 2026, measured 47 plumber websites across 15 US cities with Lighthouse audits. The average time to interactive was 1,660 milliseconds and the median was 1,243 milliseconds, meaning the typical plumber site takes well over a second before a visitor can tap a phone number or submit a form. The fastest site loaded in 255 milliseconds; the slowest took 4,981 milliseconds, nearly five full seconds. Only 13 percent of sites (6 of 47) loaded in under 500 milliseconds, while 15 percent (7 of 47) took over 3,000 milliseconds, past the point where research shows mobile users abandon pages that take longer than three seconds to load. The study's own words for that slow group: a homeowner with a burst pipe has already hit the back button. Recurring causes were render-blocking scripts (jQuery libraries, slider plugins, live chat widgets, and multiple redundant analytics tags firing at once), and several WordPress sites were still running version 4.x, which no longer receives security patches. WordPress powered 60 percent of the sites (28 of 47), and 38 percent of the 47 sites had no analytics installed at all, so those owners cannot tell whether their site generates a single lead. Separately, contractor marketer gary audie wrote on April 24, 2026 that 97 percent of contractor website visitors leave without contacting, that more than half of mobile visitors leave if a site takes over three seconds (citing Google's own data), and that contractor sites frequently hit six to ten seconds because of uncompressed hero images and bloated WordPress themes; his prescription is a load time under two seconds. The pattern a non-technical owner can recognize in five seconds: open your site on your phone and count how long until you can actually tap the number. If the page is still painting after two to three seconds, emergency callers are dialing the competitor.

## Finding 2
Colorlib opened 30 HVAC company websites in a real browser at 1280 by 860 and counted what a customer in a hurry actually encounters (the page states no publication date; the search index showed it refreshed in early September 2026). Twenty-seven of 30 had a phone number that is tappable anywhere on the page, but three sites printed the number without linking it, so tapping it on a phone does nothing. Twenty-five of 30 had the phone above the fold. Two named failures: Circle Plumbing and Heating of Alaska made the phone number and an instruction to call the largest text on the page, but the number is not a link, so tapping it does nothing; the article's verdict is that putting the phone number in the headline is the correct instinct for a cold climate and not making it tappable undoes it. Stott HVAC Systems had no clickable phone number in the opening screen at all, described as the one thing that should never be missing on a trade page. Twenty of 30 showed a Book Now or schedule button, but only eight had an actual booking system behind it; the article states that 12 companies promise scheduling they cannot deliver, while the working ones run Acuity, ServiceTitan, or Schedule Engine. Only two of 30 put a quote form above the fold, still the best result across the four trades the series measured. Contractor marketer gary audie wrote on April 24, 2026 that more than 60 percent of contractor leads still come by phone and advises the phone number in the top right of every page, tap-to-call on mobile, visible in the hero call-to-action area, and repeated in the footer, oversized: 'Treat your phone number like it's a billboard. Because to a homeowner in pain, it is.' PC Tech Magazine's local SEO guide (the page states no publication date) reports heatmap and session-recording findings that ranked pages still lose callers because the phone number sits too low for most mobile users to scroll to, reviews and trust signals are buried below service descriptions, and the primary call button is not visible above the fold on mobile; its advice is that moving a click-to-call button higher costs nothing and can improve call rates measurably. The pattern a non-technical owner can recognize: open your site on your phone and tap your own number. If it does not start a call, or if you have to scroll to find it, the site is losing the call it was built to win.

## Finding 3
Foxxr Digital Marketing's 2026 Home Service AI Visibility Report, announced August 14, 2026, audited 50 home service businesses across 12 states and 15 trades with Local Falcon geospatial grid tracking. Eighty-six percent appeared in a Google AI Overview for their core service, but only 74 percent held enough coverage across their local grid to represent actual commercial presence, and seven businesses (14 percent) did not appear once for any homeowner asking about their primary service from anywhere in their service area. The striking case: founder Brian Childers said the result that surprised the team most was 'a business with 181 reviews and a 4.8-star rating scoring zero on AI visibility.' The factors separating high scorers from low were Google Business Profile completeness, service page specificity, entity consistency across indexed sources, and third-party validation beyond directory listings; reviews alone did not carry a business. Contractor marketer gary audie wrote on April 24, 2026 that homeowners are trying to rule a contractor out in the first five seconds, and that static testimonials such as 'John S. — Great job!' no longer convert because homeowners assume they could be fabricated; his fix is to embed a live Google review feed on every key page and to show the Google rating and review count above the fold, alongside the license number, an insured or bonded badge, and years in business. PC Tech Magazine's local SEO guide (the page states no publication date) reports heatmap and session-recording findings that reviews and trust signals sit buried below service descriptions on many ranked pages. The pattern a non-technical owner can recognize: if your reviews live only on your Google listing and a visitor deciding in five seconds never sees them on your site, the trust that would have won the call never enters the room.

## Finding 4
Unitmask's guide, published August 27, 2026, names the exact failure mode: 'Copy page → replace Dallas with Plano → publish → repeat 25 times' produces 25 variations of essentially the same page, not 25 useful local resources. Its concrete example is three pages titled 'Roofing Contractor in Plano, TX,' 'Roofing Contractor in Frisco, TX,' and 'Roofing Contractor in McKinney, TX,' each reading, 'We provide reliable residential and commercial roofing services in [city]. Our experienced team delivers high-quality roofing solutions…'; the guide's verdict is that changing one city name does not create three meaningful pages, and Google's current spam policies specifically warn against substantially similar pages built to rank for similar searches, a pattern associated with doorway abuse. The owner's self-test from the guide: remove the city name from the introduction and read it again; if that paragraph could appear on every other city page, it is not localized enough. What earns a page instead: real projects from that area (project type, location, scope, before-and-after photos), reviews from customers who name the town, the services actually offered there, and named neighborhoods written naturally, since a paragraph like 'Looking for Frisco contractors? Our Frisco construction company provides Frisco construction services to Frisco homeowners needing a Frisco contractor…' makes the page worse. PC Tech Magazine's local SEO guide (the page states no publication date) adds the companion failure: a homepage is not a location page, and a generic 'we serve the greater [city] area' line in the footer does almost nothing for local organic search; dedicated pages for each service in each target area rank for queries like 'Emergency Plumber in [Neighborhood]' in a way no other page can replicate, and those long-tail searches convert at significantly higher rates. The pattern a non-technical owner can recognize: if your towns only appear in the footer sentence 'We proudly serve Dallas, Plano, Frisco, McKinney and surrounding areas,' Google does not know you are relevant to any one of them, and one strong page per town you can prove you work in beats 25 copied ones.
