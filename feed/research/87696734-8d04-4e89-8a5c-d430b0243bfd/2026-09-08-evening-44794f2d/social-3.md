Beat: social
Angle: What home service contractors and shop owners are posting this week about missed calls, after-hours coverage, and AI phone answering: real complaints, wins, skepticism, and price talk from the trades themselves, not from AI vendors. Nolan sells into exactly this conversation, so capture their words and what is driving them.

## Finding 1
A marketer who serves the trades is publicly advising her own clients not to make AI the front door. RudnikMedia, a marketing service for painting companies, posted a direct-to-camera video on September 3, 2026 telling painting business owners not to connect an AI receptionist as their primary phone answerer, arguing that callers quickly realize they are talking to AI, especially when the system uses artificial pauses, laughs, or attempts to sound human, and that this creates frustration. Her recommendation: use AI only after hours to collect basic information via text when no staff is available, then have a human follow up the next day. She goes further, arguing that hiring a human receptionist is more effective than running another marketing campaign, because generating new leads while losing existing callers to poor phone handling wastes money. The account is small (57 followers) but it is a trade-adjacent voice selling against the AI-receptionist hype to its own market.

## Finding 2
A real contractor's answer to the missed-call pitch was a flat no. Mark Poppen of Funky Moose Digital posted a video on September 2, 2026 recounting that he called a contractor that morning to confirm an appointment and hit a full voicemail; an hour later it was still full. He then suggested the contractor implement an AI voice receptionist, and the contractor rejected the idea because, in Poppen's words, 'it's not personal' and 'it's not human.' Poppen, who advises businesses on marketing, argues back that AI capturing leads beats dropping every call while too busy to answer, but the exchange captures the exact resistance Nolan sells against: a trades owner who would rather have a broken voicemail than a robot on the line. The post has 4 comments and Poppen has 2,300 followers.
- https://www.facebook.com/reel/28174964572163665/ (published 2026-09-02)

## Finding 3
The counter-evidence from a contractor's own phone: Tampa contractor Mathew Morin posted a video September 1, 2026 from a rooftop among HVAC condenser units, explaining that contractors lose jobs to competitors while on roofs or jobs, then demonstrating the AI receptionist he promotes. Screen recordings show it answering a missed call from a customer named Max about a non-working AC unit, booking an appointment for 8 PM, sending a confirmation text, providing a full transcript, and later auto-quoting $1,250 for a five-point inspection. He frames it as a full-time office manager for less than one shift's pay; the video drew 94 likes and 9 comments, and Morin has 7,500 followers. On the platform side, ServiceTitan, the verified trade-software account with 42,000 followers, posted an interview August 27, 2026 reporting a shop's voice-agent results: booked call revenue up 186 percent to $3,000 in one month, with call volume handled by the agent up 19 percent to over 400 calls. Note Morin is effectively promoting the product, so both halves are industry-adjacent, but the throughline is concrete numbers and transcripts rather than hype.

## Finding 4
The price floor for answering the phone is collapsing in public this week, and vendors are publishing each other's benchmarks. Calls Handled posted September 8, 2026 contrasting a second part-timer at $25,000 to $40,000 per year against its AI receptionist at $297 to $497 per month, noting you only pay for calls taken, with a live demo line at (561) 216-7054. Ringside AI Reception posted an infographic the same day citing ZipRecruiter Dallas-TX data from May 2026: a receptionist at $17.62 per hour costs $3,055 per month, or $36,658 per year before payroll tax, for 40 of 168 weekly hours, versus Ringside at $397 per month all-in for 24/7 coverage, a claimed $31,894 yearly difference. Meanwhile a competing argument from Elite Height Consulting, posted September 5, 2026, relays anecdotal feedback from a friend in home services that customers complained an AI tool, thought to be YC-backed Broccoli, sounded robotic and 'doesn't talk back properly'; the poster argues human virtual assistants from the Philippines at $3 per hour, covering overnight shifts across time zones, cost roughly the same as AI API credits at $20 to $30 per month, but customers prefer talking to people. Takeaway for Nolan: the price war is real, but so is the trust gap, and trade voices are pricing both.
