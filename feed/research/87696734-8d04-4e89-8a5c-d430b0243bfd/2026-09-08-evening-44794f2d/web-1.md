The stake is Nolan's: he sells AI voice reception to home service businesses, so rule changes directly shape what he can offer and how he must frame it.

## Finding 1
Pennsylvania Governor Josh Shapiro signed Senate Bill 992 on July 20, 2026, and the amendments to the Telemarketer Registration Act take effect on October 19, 2026. The reworked law replaces telephone solicitation calls with telephone solicitations covering calls, voicemails, ringless voicemails, and text messages, and it redefines robocall as any telephone solicitation using an automated dialing system to deliver prerecorded or artificial voice calls or messages, language the analysis says captures AI-generated voice calls. Robocalls now require prior express written consent, defined as a written agreement identifying the telephone number authorized, containing a clear and conspicuous disclosure that the party is consenting to receive solicitations including robocalls or texts, and signed by the called party, with an electronic signature acceptable under the federal E-SIGN Act. Verbal consent and implied consent from a prior business relationship no longer suffice for robocalls. The bill adds a new prohibited category of AI and Synthetic Messaging Fraud, barring use of any synthetic or computer-generated messaging to defraud, deceive, or mislead residential, business, or wireless subscribers, a provision read as targeting AI voice cloning and deepfake audio in scam calls. Calling hours tighten: no solicitations on Sundays, before 9 a.m., or after 7 p.m., with quiet-hour suppression keyed to the recipient's location rather than the caller's time zone. Penalties are civil fines of up to $1,000 per violation, rising to $3,000 per violation when the consumer is 60 or older, and violations also count under Pennsylvania's Unfair Trade Practices and Consumer Protection Law, which carries a private right of action.

## Finding 2
On September 8, 2026, the FCC announced that its September open meeting agenda includes an order and further notice of proposed rulemaking aimed at strengthening consumers' ability to revoke consent for robocalls and robotexts. The item would take a balanced approach to the revoke all rule, drawing on a joint proposal in the record from consumer groups and industry stakeholders, while preserving wanted calls such as fraud alerts. The announcement frames the goal as giving consumers more choice: people who revoke marketing-message consent should still receive the calls they do want. The Commission had not voted on the item as of the announcement; it is a meeting item this month.

## Finding 3
On August 13, 2026, a judge in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California allowed BIPA claims to proceed in In re Otter.ai Privacy Litig., No. 5:25-cv-06911, a class action against the AI video-call recording and transcription service. Plaintiffs allege the Otter Notetaker joins virtual meetings automatically, records and transcribes speech without consent, and retains voiceprints and other personal information for commercial purposes. The court found that Otter.ai's speaker-tagging features plausibly involve capturing voiceprints, which BIPA counts as biometric identifiers, and rejected Otter's motion to dismiss for lack of Article III standing and failure to state a claim, quoting the holding that plaintiffs plausibly allege Otter violated section 15(b) by capturing their voiceprints during virtual meetings. The court did not decide the merits. BIPA requires notice and written consent before collection, plus a retention schedule and destruction guidelines, and provides a private right of action with statutory damages of $1,000 per negligent violation, $5,000 per intentional or reckless violation, or actual damages, whichever is greater. The analysis warns that liability may reach not only AI transcription vendors but also companies that use their services, and advises due diligence on call-recordation vendors.

## Finding 4
Florida Attorney General James Uthmeier announced on Tuesday, September 8, 2026, in Tampa that he is proposing legislation creating criminal penalties for corporations when their AI chatbots engage in crimes. Potential sanctions named include significant fines, payments to victims, court-ordered monitorship, and in some cases suspension of a business after conviction. Uthmeier said companies must be held accountable because they design, control, and profit from the technology, and added that the framework could extend beyond AI companies to social media companies whose use of the technology results in criminal conduct. The proposal is in its early stages, with his office still working with lawmakers on details; he called it not a ban on AI. At the same event, Uthmeier said his office continues a criminal investigation and civil lawsuit against OpenAI, with another round of subpoenas expected within about a week, and Florida Department of Law Enforcement Commissioner Mark Glass said investigators are seeing criminals use AI for voice cloning and malware, along with scam calls originating from correctional facilities.
