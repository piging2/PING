## Finding 1
Google is changing its Local Services Ads lead charge policy effective October 1, 2026, in a way that puts missed calls directly on a contractor's ad bill. The change was announced in an email Google sent to advertisers at the end of August 2026. Under the new valid-lead criteria, if a potential customer calls a business through a Local Services Ad during its listed business hours, stays on the line for more than 20 seconds, and nobody answers, Google can count that missed call as a billable lead and charge for it. Follow-up calls also matter: if the first call does not qualify as a charged lead but a later call between that customer and the business meets Google's valid-lead criteria, the later call can still be charged. There is an IVR exception: when a business's inbound setup requires the caller to press a key to route the call, the 20-second timer starts only when the caller presses the key, and no charge applies if the caller never presses a key. Mediagistic notes this creates a distinction between IVR and other automated answering technology, including AI answering services and auto-attendants, where a system technically answering the call is not the same as connecting the homeowner with someone who can help. Responsiveness also feeds Google's LSA auction ranking: missed calls may negatively affect responsiveness, so a contractor can pay twice, once for the missed lead and again through weaker ad ranking. Mediagistic's recommended pre-October-1 audit includes reviewing listed business hours, testing call routing, reviewing IVR configuration, evaluating missed-call coverage including AI answering coverage, and secret-shopping after-hours answering services. For the software playbook, grade the announcement itself CONFIRMED (reported by Mediagistic on September 10, 2026 and independently corroborated by an August 2026 Medium analysis citing Search Engine Land's coverage of the same policy). Grade the IVR exception detail as STRONG INDICATION (it rests on Mediagistic's reading of Google's advertiser email as reported via HomePros, not on Google's public documentation). The timing is directly relevant to the pitch: starting October 1, a contractor without reliable call coverage pays Google for calls nobody answered.

## Finding 2
On August 26, 2026, Dispatchly AI announced Dispatchly Rescue, a switch incentive aimed squarely at Jobber customers in home services. Dispatchly describes itself as an AI-native platform combining front office, field operations, and customer communication in one system, including an AI receptionist that answers calls and books jobs, plus estimates, photo estimates, invoicing, scheduling, business IVR, AI speed-to-lead, marketing, payroll, and an AI-built website. Under the Rescue program, eligible U.S. home-service businesses get 12 months of the Dispatchly Intro plan free. Eligibility required a paid Jobber subscription for at least 12 consecutive months; the business activates Dispatchly via standard checkout with a valid credit card, cancels the prior subscription within 30 calendar days of activation, and submits dated proof of the prior subscription and cancellation from Settings to Billing, reviewed within five business days. No paid subscription begins automatically when the free period ends; the customer must actively select a paid plan. Founder David Liu positioned it against tool sprawl, saying small contractors are paying for a scheduling tool, then paying again for a separate answering service, then paying again for an AI add-on, and none of it talks to each other. For the software playbook, grade the program terms CONFIRMED as announced (this is Dispatchly's own press release, carried verbatim by EIN Presswire on August 26, 2026). Grade actual contractor uptake and savings as UNKNOWN (no customer numbers or results were stated).
