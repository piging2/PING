## Finding 1
The two bounces from the paper's earlier coverage, fleursauvage.llc@gmail.com and info@denver-appliance.repair on September 10 and 11, both returned 550 5.1.1. That code means the recipient address does not exist: Intercom's help table states that 550-5.1.1 typically means the email address being sent to is not valid, and that the recipient may have closed the account or there may be a typo. A 5.x.x code is permanent, refused outright and never retried, and is recorded as a hard bounce. So this is a recipient-side list problem, not a sender-side deliverability problem: nothing about the sending domain, the message content, or the sending volume caused these. The fix the providers themselves prescribe is to cut dead addresses. Google's sender guidelines say to automatically unsubscribe recipients who have multiple bounced messages, and Yahoo's Sender Hub says to remove invalid recipients from the list promptly. The verdict for tonight's sends: drop both addresses from the prospect list now, never retry a 5.1.1, and eyeball each new address for typos before it goes out, since Google also advises confirming each recipient's email address. (The Intercom help page states its last update only as '2 days ago' rather than a calendar date; the Google and Yahoo pages state no publication date.)

## Finding 2
No new Google or Yahoo bulk-sender requirement changes were published this week. Google's own 'Sender requirements updates' table on its Email sender guidelines page lists exactly one change in its history: the TLS-connection requirement, dated December 2023. Nothing from 2026 appears. Yahoo's Sender Hub requirements are likewise unchanged from the February 2024 enforcement rollout, and Yahoo says the requirements are subject to change and points senders to its blog and that page for updates. The standing rules, from the two providers' own pages: every sender needs SPF or DKIM authentication, valid forward and reverse DNS, a TLS connection, and spam complaint rates below 0.3%. Bulk senders, defined by Google as more than 5,000 messages per day to personal Gmail accounts, additionally need SPF and DKIM together, a DMARC record with a policy of at least p=none, From-domain alignment with the authenticated domain, and one-click unsubscribe on marketing mail. A sender at up to 10 emails a day sits nowhere near the 5,000-a-day bulk threshold, so the all-sender set is what binds, and sending through an authenticated Gmail account covers the authentication side. (The Google and Yahoo pages state no publication date; the Mailercloud 2026 reference states it was last reviewed on 10 August 2026.)

## Finding 3
The week's actual Gmail sending news is the Gmail Verified Sender Program, which launched September 8, 2026, but it is not a change to ordinary sender rules and does not apply to a small business sender. The program is limited to eligible U.S. political committees: candidates, parties, PACs, and other registered committees. To take part, they must verify their sending domains and meet Google's security, authentication, and compliance rules, including keeping their user-reported spam rate below 0.3% over a rolling 14-day average; a sender above that rate violates the policy and can be suspended or removed. A Google spokesperson said that to participate, 'bulk senders must meet strict security and compliance requirements, ensuring that messages people receive are relevant, useful, and verified.' The direction of travel at Google remains tighter enforcement for everyone else, not looser.
